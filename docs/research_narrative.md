# Part I: The Study — Subject, Literature, Design, and Plan

## Prologue: the language and why it matters

To understand what this study translates — and why the choice of subject is unusually well-grounded — one must start with MUMPS itself. MUMPS (Massachusetts General Hospital Utility Multi-Programming System) was born in 1966 at Massachusetts General Hospital on a DEC PDP-7, created by Neil Pappalardo, Robert Greenes, and Curt Marble. It descends from an identifiable lineage of interactive languages — JOSS (RAND, 1963) begat TELCOMP (BBN, 1964), whose string-processing variant STRINGCOMP (1967) shaped MUMPS's deep integration of language and database. The language was standardized early and repeatedly: ANSI X11.1 in 1977, 1980, 1984, 1990, and 1995, and as ISO 11756 in 1992 and 1999 — a standardization pedigree few languages of its vintage can claim. Its academic evaluation began almost immediately: Wasserman and Sherertz's 1976 "A Balanced View of MUMPS" concluded that standard MUMPS was "sufficiently rich to implement real-world applications mostly without resorting to vendor-specific extensions," and the MUMPS Development Committee's technical reports (MDC 1/3 on the language specification, MDC 2/1 on the globals mechanism — the hierarchical sparse-array storage at the heart of the language — and MDC 2/3 on standard implementation) constitute an unusually complete formal record for a 1970s systems language.

What makes MUMPS more than a historical curiosity is that it never left production. The project's bibliography, built by citation pearl growing from core sources (a method documented in Markey & Atherton's 1978 ONTAP manual and Hawkins & Wagers' 1982 paper), traces a remarkable industrial diaspora: Epic Systems, whose MUMPS/Caché platform touches records for over 78% of U.S. patients; MEDITECH, founded by Pappalardo himself; InterSystems (Caché/IRIS, whose technology the European Space Agency chose in 2010 for the Gaia star-mapping mission); the VA's VistA, one of the most successful EHR implementations ever deployed, and its military sibling CHCS; laboratory systems across Sunquest, Quest Diagnostics, Dynacare, and DASA; the Bank of England, Barclays, and Ameritrade's twelve-billion-transaction-per-day trading platform in finance; even a veterinary adaptation (VMACS at UC Davis) and library automation systems from DataPhase's ALIS through OCLC's LS/2000. Modern open implementations — GT.M (open-sourced on Linux in 2000), its fork YottaDB, FreeM, RSM — keep the language alive today. This is the stakes-setting for the study: the artifact being translated is not a toy, but a representative instance of a language family that still runs hospitals, banks, and a space telescope's data pipeline.

The modernization question is also already on record. The VA itself studied converting MUMPS to Java (2009); Newcomb and Couch documented the VA's VistA MUMPS modernization pilot in 2010; and Diggs et al. (arXiv:2411.14971, 2024) showed recently that LLM-generated documentation for a real MUMPS codebase (WorldVistA) was largely hallucination-free — evidence that MUMPS aligns with modern language constructs better than its reputation suggests. Zaytsev's "Open Challenges in Incremental Coverage of Legacy Software Languages" (PX/17) names precisely the difficulties this study confronts: poor documentation, large scale, buggy reference implementations, fragile workarounds. The study's literature review also records, with scholarly honesty, a bibliographic hazard: searching "MUMPS" on health-science preprint servers returns the infectious disease.

## The subject: what the source actually is

The study's chosen artifact is Dr. Kevin C. O'Kane's **Mumps II Interpreter-Compiler Library** — one component of his MUMPS distribution tarball (the 190 MB `mumps-14-may-2026.tgz`, SHA-256 recorded at fetch time), distinct from the legacy interpreter also shipped in the distribution. Inspecting the source confirms and enriches the design's description:

- **The compiler is C** (~8,700 lines across 21 `compiler-*.c` files — the parser `compiler-parse.c` at 2,508 lines and the driver `compiler-mumps.c` at 1,998 being the largest), headed by O'Kane's GPL-v2+ copyright block spanning 2003–2026, with the interpreter files carrying LGPL notices. The `mumpsc` wrapper drives compilation, and the build produces `mumps2c` as the interpreter binary.
- **The interpreter is C++** — ~26,000 lines across `interp-*.cpp` plus the 10,577-line `libmpscpp.cpp`, a runtime whose own header describes it as usable for indirection in compiled code. The interpreter half carries distinctive subsystems that materially shape the translation problem: a GTK GUI wrapper layer (154 GTK references each in `interp-zfcns.cpp` and `interp-bifs.cpp`), an SQL/RDBMS interface (`interp-sql.cpp`, `rdbms-if.c`, a SQLite-backed globals database with configurable journal modes), PCRE pattern matching (vendored), and a native B-tree option for global storage.
- **Total: 48 top-level C/C++ files and 41 headers (~4,700 lines) under `include/mumpsc/`**, roughly 38,000 lines of translatable top-level source, built for Linux x86_64 via a vendor `configure`+`make` with autoconf `.in` templates, packaging payloads, and an SVN `Checkout/` of demo programs that the study's corpus definition explicitly excludes.

The design document (§2, corrected in v1.2a) commits to this exact scope, and to an important consequence the earlier drafts missed: because the interpreter half is C++, this is honestly a **dual-language** study — C→Kotlin *and* C++→Kotlin — with every sampled unit language-tagged (96 C and 212 C++ of the 308 selected function units) and pre-registered subgroup analyses S1–S3 to report per-language outcomes while primary contrasts stay pooled.

## The intellectual problem and its literature

The study asks two linked questions that the literature has not jointly answered. First, what does formal verification buy in legacy translation? Second, what does making translation knowledge *explicit and reusable* buy — and which of its ingredients is responsible?

**On translation and verification**, the review assembles a methodological toolkit: Han et al.'s LLM-driven translation pipeline (type annotation, semantic description with resampling, refinement); Cforall's demonstration that evolutionary source-to-source extension is viable; Cassol and Arévalo's M2K methodology for inferring object models from procedural C (the procedural-to-OO bridge Kotlin requires); Ge et al.'s correct-by-construction Event-B-to-C pipeline with equivalence proofs; and on the verification side, Dafny's Boogie-to-Z3 architecture, SafeOSL's ownership-based memory-safety verification, and a growing Dafny research line — Dafny as a verification-aware intermediate language for code generation (Li et al. 2025), LLM-assisted proof authoring (Silva et al. 2024; Wesselink et al. 2026), and even a fully verified Dafny compiler (Nezamabadi et al., CakeML backend). Industrial scale is represented by Schuts et al.'s semi-automated migration of 100,000+ lines of legacy C/C++ test code using Clang-based metaprogramming (ClangMR, TXL, DMS, Stratego/XT).

**On declarative heuristic capture**, the review establishes a long pedigree: Warren's 1980 demonstration that Prolog is a natural medium for compiler writing (grammars as definite clauses); Bowen's 1993 Prolog compiler *and decompiler* — the same declarative rule base run in both directions, implying that a captured heuristic base is bidirectional, reusable for reverse-engineering and debugging, not just translation; Dossis and Barkaoui's industrial synthesis pipeline where C-like source becomes Prolog facts from which rules conclude output models correct-by-construction; Pau and Kristinsson's SOFTM maintenance expert system; and Ward and Bennett's 1995 argument that formal transformation rules are the only economically viable route for legacy migration at scale. The closest published analogue to this project is Sampaio do Prado Leite et al.'s transformational COBOL port with a reusable rule library — whose "rule libraries amortize heuristic-discovery cost" insight is the central economic hypothesis of the study's PRO arm.

Three further threads complete the theoretical scaffolding. From the term-rewriting community: Balland's Tom system separating rules from strategy, Bissyandé's Coccinelle-based embedded compiler (translation knowledge as a small-grained, auditable rule base), and — cited directly in the design's preprocessing section — Newman et al.'s finding that canonicalization before rule application collapses many syntactic guises of one semantic situation into a single rule, which is why the study inserts a canonicalization step before heuristic capture. From the LLM era: Wang et al. (2026) showing that translation decisions made *inside* an LLM are opaque and non-reusable, and — as sharp industrial corroboration — Google's internal code-migration pipelines (deterministic "edit recipes," LLMs generating the recipes) and Bloomberg's hybrid LLM-deterministic migration pipelines, both converging on architectures where durable, inspectable transformation knowledge complements the model. From the logic-program verification tradition: De Angelis et al. verifying imperative programs *by transformation into constraint logic programs*, and Drabent's "logic + control" principle — mirrored by Balland and Newman — which the design invokes to keep the Prolog rule base's logic distinct from its application strategy. The COBOL-modernization wave (IRONPROOF's SMT-based equivalence checking; Kumar et al.'s automated COBOL-to-Java validation; Froimovich et al.'s quality metrics; SEDCoT's symbolic-execution enhancement) supplies the metric and validation templates the study adapts.

The monographic shelf supports the craft side: O'Kane's own *The MUMPS Programming Language* (2008) as authoritative language reference; Lewkowicz's *Complete MUMPS*; Jemerov & Isakova's *Kotlin in Action* and Saumont's *Joy of Kotlin* for the target language; Moore's *Compiler Design Using Kotlin* — an object-oriented approach to exactly this kind of build; and the design-patterns canon (Gamma et al., Fowler) for the OO mapping work.

**The gap, stated in the review with unusual candor:** no published study compares machine-assisted translation with and without declaratively captured, reusable heuristics — let alone in combination with formal verification, on a single well-defined legacy artifact. The study's contribution is to build that comparison *and* to decompose it.

## The experimental design

The design is a **partial 2×3 factorial** over two factors: formal verification (absent/Dafny) and heuristic handling (ad hoc-logged / written cookbook / machine-queried Prolog rules). Five arms are run; the Dafny×cookbook cell is deliberately omitted (it becomes a follow-up only if an interaction appears):

- **BASE** — no verification, ad hoc heuristics (logged). The control, the only full-codebase arm, and the source of the frozen oracle.
- **DOC** — a Markdown cookbook, manually consulted. The design's most thoughtful addition: it exists *solely* to decompose the Prolog effect, because "capturing heuristics in Prolog" bundles three ingredients — the discipline of writing them down, the declarative representation, and machine consultation. DOC holds discipline constant and strips the rest, so DOC vs BASE measures discipline and PRO vs DOC isolates representation-plus-consultation.
- **PRO** — an SWI-Prolog rule base, machine-queried during translation, with `find_rule/1`, `record_reuse/2`, `coverage_report/0` tooling; every translated construct traceable to a rule or a logged one-off.
- **DAF** — BASE's workflow plus simultaneous Dafny specification and proof.
- **BOTH** — Dafny plus the Prolog rule base, with rules constraining the Kotlin before verification and verification failures feeding back into the rules (a rule whose target repeatedly fails verification is amended, with the amendment recorded — a *verification-aware* rule base, echoing the correct-by-construction traditions of Ge and Dossis).

Four primary contrasts are pre-registered: verification (DAF vs BASE), documentation discipline (DOC vs BASE), declarative mechanism (PRO vs DOC), and the interaction as a difference-of-differences, (BOTH − DAF) vs (PRO − BASE). The hypotheses predict: BASE translates 80–90% with heavy manual intervention on pointers, memory management, and macros; DAF reaches ~95%+ correctness at 2–3× effort; DOC buys modest consistency; PRO beats DOC in later batches as rules mature (H10 predicts >50% reuse for constructs appearing in 3+ modules, with the cookbook a subset of what the rule base enforces mechanically); BOTH costs most but sub-additively, with the highest correctness and auditability.

Because every arm translates the same modules, module is a blocking factor — a within-subjects design where each module yields a matched observation per arm.

## Protecting the study from its single author

One researcher translating everything over 18 months creates two mortal threats: learning effects and grading drift. The design answers with four pre-commitments, all already committed to the repository before any translation work:

1. **Frozen oracle.** The original system is built and characterized before any Kotlin exists; all five arms are graded forever against that unchanging suite. A 15-case deterministic characterization harness (covering operators, `$Piece`/`$Extract`/`$Justify`/`$FNumber`, globals with `$Order`/`$Query`/`Merge`, pattern matching, indirection/`Xecute`, user functions, `New` scoping) is written; the freeze happens on the reference build.
2. **Latin-square counterbalancing.** The Study Subset — 308 function units, complexity-stratified (102/102/104), selected by a seeded reproducible procedure (seed 20261113, committed with per-file SHA-256 of every scanned source) into 10 balanced batches — is translated by every arm, each in a different pre-assigned cyclic order (BASE starts at B1, DOC at B2, …, BOTH at B5), with the "freshest hours" rotating among arms each cycle. The design's own history is instructive here: the earlier sequential schedule would have made "Prolog helps" indistinguishable from "the researcher got better"; the cyclic construction absorbs the learning curve into the block structure. The order assignments are committed in `wp1/order_assignments.json`, dated 2026-11-14, and are never revised — deviations are logged as data.
3. **Pre-registered capture criteria.** Identical for DOC and PRO, committed before the pilot: capture triggers (recurrence ≥2, or >15 minutes' resolution effort, or external-reference consultation); a shared entry schema (trigger, condition, action, example, provenance, verification status); four quality gates (generality beyond the source unit; canonical form; no oracle contamination; deduplication); and exact reuse semantics — hit, reuse, adapt, miss — with metric definitions (capture rate, hit rate, reuse rate, coverage) fixed in advance.
4. **Micro-replication.** Three units per arm, drawn from per-arm seeded streams and committed, are re-translated at the end — an honest within-arm variance estimate that openly acknowledges single-researcher pseudo-replication. Per-module measures are the unit of analysis; inferential tests are exploratory support, not definitive proof.

## The plan: 18 months, six work packages, seven milestones

**WP1 (Nov–Dec 2026)** prepares everything: environments (Kotlin, Dafny, Z3, SWI-Prolog, Clang tooling), preprocessing and canonicalization of the source, the baseline build and oracle freeze, stratification, the committed order assignments, capture criteria, and the micro-replication draws. **WP2 (Jan 2027)** is the pilot batch, translated five ways, seeding cookbook v0 and rule base v0 from the pilot's heuristic log under identical criteria, and calibrating instrumentation. **WP3 (Feb 2027–Jan 2028)** is the long interleaved middle: monthly cycles where each arm advances one batch through its order, knowledge artifacts are versioned with provenance, verification closes each batch in DAF/BOTH, and BASE additionally translates the non-study remainder. **WP4 (Feb–Mar 2028)** re-translates the micro-replication units and completes comprehensive testing, including differential testing against the C baseline and mutation testing of the suite. **WP5 (Apr 2028)** estimates the contrasts with blocked models (batch position and artifact maturity as covariates), verifies the learning-curve analysis confirms counterbalancing worked, and synthesizes qualitatively. **WP6 (May 2028)** delivers the final report. Milestones gate the path: M1 (Dec 31, 2026, oracle frozen and batches committed) through M7 (May 31, 2028, project complete). Total effort is budgeted at ~1,400–1,600 hours across roughly 20–25 hours per week.

## What is measured, and what it means

Six metric dimensions are collected identically in every arm: translation effectiveness; code quality (including *cross-module decision consistency* — the same construct must receive the same translation everywhere, the metric DOC and PRO are hypothesized to move); effort, including the maintenance cost of the knowledge artifacts themselves; verification statistics (proof obligations, iterations per function — the interaction metric); heuristic capture and reuse (growth curves, hit rates, auditability); and learning/process data. The analysis framework pairs the four primary contrasts with pre-registered language subgroups (S1 per-language BASE difficulty; S2 arm × language interactions; S3 batch c/cpp mix as covariate), secondary pairwise contrasts, and cost-per-line and cost-per-defect-prevented economics — specifically the marginal cost of the Prolog mechanism over the cookbook, the design's distinctive comparison.

The deliverables are substantial: five Kotlin translations of the same legacy system; a provenance-complete Prolog rule base and cookbook; heuristic decision logs; the frozen oracle; a metrics database; and a research paper — *"Comparative Study of Machine-Assisted C-to-Kotlin Translation: Formal Verification and Declarative Heuristic Capture in a Five-Arm Design"* — aimed at the software engineering, formal methods, and logic programming communities. The deeper ambition is an *explanation* rather than a winner: a controlled decomposition of why knowledge capture helps (discipline vs. representation vs. machine consultation), and the first measurement of whether a reusable heuristic base changes the cost or the value of formal verification.

# Part II: Measurement, Analysis, and the Limits of What This Study Can Claim

## The numeric measures

The design's metrics framework spans six dimensions, each with concrete numbers collected per module, per batch, per arm, and across the study.

**Translation effectiveness.** Percentage of each unit translated by machine assistance before first human edit; manual intervention rate (human edits per 100 LOC, counted from git diffs between the machine's initial translation and the completed unit); translation accuracy at the unit level (fraction of frozen-oracle cases attached to that unit that pass on first run); and overall coverage (percent of the Study Subset completed per arm, with deferrals reported separately and never silently dropped).

**Code quality.** Defect density (defects per KLOC, where a defect is any divergence from oracle-observed behavior found by the characterization suite, differential testing against the C baseline, mutation-amplified tests, or review); test coverage (JaCoCo line and branch percentages per unit); cyclomatic complexity and Halstead metrics computed identically on the C source and the Kotlin output (the *complexity delta* — how much the translation itself changed the program's measured complexity — is itself a study outcome); a maintainability index per arm; and, distinctively, **cross-module translation-decision consistency**, operationalized as: for each pair of canonical constructs that received the same classification under the capture criteria, the fraction translated to the same Kotlin idiom across modules and batches — a number computable in every arm (from decision logs) but expected to differ sharply between ad hoc and captured arms.

**Effort.** Wall-clock time per unit and per batch from session-level time tracking; number of edit-compile-test iterations per unit; developer hours per KLOC translated (the headline effort unit for cost-per-line economics); and — often ignored in comparable studies — the separately bookkept maintenance overhead of the knowledge artifacts themselves: cookbook upkeep minutes and rule-base capture minutes (per rule, per amendment), so that the PRO arm's total cost honestly includes the cost of building its own tool.

**Verification (DAF, BOTH).** Proof obligations generated per unit; verification success rate (obligations discharged / generated); verification wall-clock time per unit; iterations per function (times a proof had to be re-attempted after spec or code changes); and deferral count and deferral rate (units reclassified verification-deferred, per the contingency plan).

**Heuristic capture and reuse (DOC, PRO, BOTH).** Rule-base and cookbook size over time (entries/rules at each cycle — the growth curve); hit rate (consultations ÷ triggering situations); reuse rate (rules/entries applied unmodified ÷ hits); adaptation count; miss rate; capture rate (entries created ÷ situations meeting capture triggers); artifact maturity at time of each unit's translation (entries + cumulative reuse count, logged as the covariate); auditability (fraction of translated constructs traceable to a recorded rule, entry, or logged decision); and in BOTH specifically, verification-triggered amendments per rule (the verification-awareness signal).

**Learning and process.** Batch position vs. effort (the learning curve fitted per arm); order-assignment deviations (count and description, logged per cycle, with a success criterion of ≤10% out-of-order translations); and within-arm variance from micro-replication (3 modules × 2 passes × 5 arms = 30 paired observations).

**Data collection mechanics** are specified per metric: cloc for LOC, git commits for manual changes, JaCoCo for coverage, session tracking for time, decision logs for heuristics, Dafny logs for verification, and the cycle log for position/deviation data — all in CSV/JSON databases version-controlled alongside the code.

## The statistical methods

The analysis plan is deliberately conservative in its inferential claims and specific about its machinery.

**Primary contrasts (H2–H5).** Because the design is within-subjects — every arm translates the same 308 units — the four primary contrasts are computed as **paired per-module comparisons**: for each unit, the outcome difference between the two contrasted arms, aggregated across units. For approximately normal outcome differences, paired t-tests with per-module pairing; for skewed distributions (effort and defects will be right-skewed), the **Wilcoxon signed-rank** test on paired differences. Every contrast is reported with an **effect size** (paired standardized mean difference, analogous to Cohen's d for paired designs), a 95% confidence interval, and — because the four primary contrasts are a pre-specified family — a **Holm–Bonferroni step-down adjustment** over the four, with raw p-values always shown alongside adjusted ones.

**H5, the interaction**, is a **difference-in-differences**: for each unit, compute (BOTH − DAF) and (PRO − BASE) on the outcome, then test whether these two per-unit quantities differ — again paired, since both differences are computed on the same unit. This construction keeps the interaction test on the same within-unit footing as the main effects.

**Blocked models.** Beyond the univariate contrasts, outcomes are modeled with **blocked/mixed-effect models** at the unit level: outcome ~ arm + batch position + artifact maturity at translation time + (1 | unit) — unit as a random effect (or equivalently a fixed block, given the one observation per arm-unit cell), batch position as the learning covariate, and artifact maturity as a time-varying covariate. For count-type outcomes (defects, interventions) the corresponding Poisson or negative-binomial GLMM is used instead of the linear model. The model's coefficient on batch position doubles as the H11 test: if counterbalancing worked, position predicts effort (learning happens) but does not differentially predict it *by arm* — the arm × position interaction is the formal check that no factor level was confounded with calendar time.

**Learning-curve analysis (H11).** Effort ~ batch position, fitted per arm; the design's adequacy criterion is that the position term is absorbed by the block structure and the arm × position interaction is not significant. A significant interaction is itself reported as a design finding, not hidden.

**Within-arm variance.** The micro-replication observations (3 units re-translated per arm) yield per-arm, per-metric within-researcher variance estimates; these are reported next to every contrast estimate so the reader can see within-arm noise alongside between-arm effect. This is a variance calibration, not a power calculation — the design does not pretend otherwise.

**H6 (pooled corners).** Verified arms (DAF, BOTH) vs non-verified (BASE, DOC, PRO) on defect/edge-case counts: Mann–Whitney U on module-level counts, reported as secondary.

**Consistency and reuse.** Consistency is compared across arms with the paired machinery on the per-unit consistency scores; reuse-rate comparison between DOC and PRO uses the logged lookup/hit records (the instrumentation guarantees comparability by definition — both arms log hits under identical criteria). Growth curves (artifact size, cumulative reuse) are plotted per cycle and compared descriptively, with the maturity covariate carrying the inferential weight in the blocked models. Correlation analyses (reuse rate vs. manual intervention rate) are explicitly exploratory.

**Pre-registered subgroups (S1–S3).** The four primary contrasts re-estimated within `lang` (C vs. C++) with arm × language interaction terms; batch c/cpp composition as a covariate in the blocked models. Subgroup results are secondary and reported regardless of outcome.

**Qualitative analysis.** Alongside the numbers: challenge categorization from decision logs, pattern identification across batches, and a narrative of rule-base/cookbook evolution — the explanatory layer for whatever the quantitative contrasts show, per the sequential explanatory (QUAN → qual) design.

## Sources of error, actual and potential — and their mitigations

A study like this is more threat-model than lab protocol; the design identifies its error sources explicitly.

**Measurement error in effort.** Self-timed, self-reported hours are noisy: session interruptions leak in, tasks bleed together. *Mitigation:* per-session granularity (the smallest honest unit), time tracking calibrated during the pilot, and the acknowledgment that effort noise inflates variance but not bias — the paired design cancels much of it, since all arms share the same sloppy clock.

**Grading drift.** Over 18 months, the researcher's implicit grading standard could drift. *Mitigation:* the frozen oracle — grading is byte-comparison against outputs fixed before translation began, mechanically applied, immune to drift. Oracle *sufficiency* is a separate, deeper error source (below).

**Learning confounds.** The single greatest internal-validity threat: the researcher gets better. The design's own history shows this was real — the original sequential-block schedule was abandoned precisely because it would have made the Prolog factor collinear with skill. *Mitigation:* Latin-square counterbalancing, pre-committed orders, the batch-position covariate, the arm × position interaction test, and the rotating "fresh attention" assignment. *Residual risk:* if interleaving discipline collapses (success criterion: ≤10% out-of-order), the logged position data still permits post-hoc adjustment, but the confound is weakened, not eliminated — and the design says so in advance.

**Oracle insufficiency (construct validity of "correctness").** Characterization tests capture *observed* behavior, not the full semantics: passing the frozen oracle is evidence of equivalence on tested paths, not proof of functional equivalence. *Mitigation:* differential testing against the live C baseline, mutation testing of the test suite (a weak oracle is exposed when mutants survive), property-based tests (Kotest), and honest reporting of oracle coverage. *Residual risk:* untested paths remain untested; the design scopes correctness claims to "equivalent on the frozen oracle plus extensions."

**Heuristic-subjectivity error.** What counts as a "reusable heuristic" is judgment. If DOC and PRO captured *different* heuristics, the PRO vs DOC contrast becomes a comparison of two different treatments. *Mitigation:* pre-registered capture criteria (identical triggers, schema, gates), the rule base seeded from the same pilot log, and the auditability metric — the fraction of decisions traceable to recorded rules — is itself measured, so a degraded capture is visible in the data.

**Pseudo-replication.** One researcher, one codebase: the 308 units are not independent draws from a population of translators or programs. *Mitigation:* unit-of-analysis honesty (per-module measures; tests as exploratory support), micro-replication for within-arm variance, and pre-registration so exploratory p-values cannot masquerade as confirmatory ones. The design explicitly flags this as limitation #8.

**Multiple comparisons.** Four primary contrasts plus secondary pairwise contrasts plus subgroups invite false positives. *Mitigation:* pre-specified contrast hierarchy, Holm adjustment within the primary family, and labeling everything else exploratory.

**Instrumentation reactivity.** Logging every decision may itself change behavior (the DOC/PRO arms are *told* to be consistent). *Mitigation:* all arms keep the same decision log — BASE and DAF log too — so the logging burden is common across arms and only the capture/consultation treatment differs.

**Tool and environment error.** Compiler versions, Dafny/Z3 updates, glibc float formatting changes could shift baselines mid-study. *Mitigation:* versioned environments, the freeze protocol (any oracle re-verification failure is a logged protocol deviation, never a silent re-freeze), and differential testing against the same pinned C baseline build.

**Selection error.** The Study Subset must represent the corpus, and the sampling must be honest. *Mitigation:* seeded, reproducible selection with committed per-file SHA-256 (regeneration with the same seed is verification; a different seed is a protocol deviation), complexity stratification, and balance constraints on batch size, LOC, and language mix.

**Actual errors so far — logged, not hidden.** The project's own history is instructive: the subject was initially misidentified (the tool was first tested against the wrong component of the tarball — corrected in v1.2a); the chunker shipped with a line-identity bug that inflated one unit to a phantom 10,110 LOC (fixed and re-run); the design's sequential schedule was itself a design error, corrected into the Latin square. This error-visible style is the study's best protection: its deviations are data.

## Shortcomings and limits of applicability — an honest accounting

Beyond the itemized risks (Mumps II complexity, the Dafny learning curve, verification intractability with its 50%-of-subset success criterion, capture overhead exceeding benefit, and the personal risks of a long part-time solo project — health, competing priorities), the design carries structural shortcomings that no mitigation fully removes:

1. **N = 1 in every dimension that matters.** One codebase, one researcher, one tool stack, one point in time. A Dafny effect measured here is an effect for *this* translator, on *this* code, with *this* LLM assistance, in 2027–28. Replication by others is the only real remedy, and the design can only make replication easy (frozen oracle, committed seeds, open artifacts), not perform it.
2. **The arms are not double-blind, and cannot be.** The researcher knows which arm he is in; expectation effects (Prolog *should* help) can influence effort allocation and subjective judgments. Objective metrics (oracle passes, logged hits, time) are the counterweight; subjective ones are unavoidably contaminated.
3. **Effort is not additive.** BASE translates the full codebase while other arms translate the Study Subset; the BASE remainder work happens in parallel and potentially builds skill asymmetrically. The design excludes remainder work from cross-arm analysis, but skill bleed-through cannot be excluded — the batch-position covariate absorbs only part of it.
4. **The oracle measures what the original does, bugs included.** If O'Kane's implementation has quirks (and 38,000 lines of MUMPS runtime will), "correct" translation reproduces them. This is characterization, not specification — and a Dafny-verified Kotlin that *fixes* a C bug will look *wrong* to the oracle. The study must and does treat such cases as findings, not failures.
5. **Verification coverage asymmetry.** Dafny verification of translated, garbage-collected, idiomatic Kotlin is philosophically distinct from verifying C semantics; "50% of the DAF subset verified" may mean the *hard* 50% is deferred, making DAF look better than its verification deserves. The deferral logs make this visible but do not eliminate it.
6. **Statistical power is unknown and probably modest.** 308 units × 5 arms is substantial for paired contrasts of large effects, but the design honestly declines power analysis (no defensible effect-size prior exists) — micro-replication variance will show, after the fact, which contrasts the study was capable of detecting. Small true effects may go unresolved; null results carry wide intervals rather than precise zeros.
7. **The Prolog factor is confounded with tooling maturity by construction.** PRO's benefit, if any, accrues partly from the *instrumentation* (query tooling, coverage reports) that DOC cannot mechanically have. The design's answer — DOC gets the same logged lookups, done by hand — narrows but does not close this gap; a DOC proponent could argue the comparison handicaps human lookup.
8. **Timeline fragility.** Eighteen months at 20–25 hours/week assumes uninterrupted capacity; the design's own risk table rates competing priorities and health issues as live risks. A stretched timeline stretches the learning curve the counterbalancing must absorb, weakening the design the longer it runs.

**Limits of applicability.** The results generalize most directly to: single-researcher or small-team modernization of legacy C/C++ systems of roughly 10–100 KLOC, where the translator controls both source understanding and target craft; to garbage-collected, statically-typed OO targets (Kotlin, less so Rust, where memory semantics dominate); and to codebases whose behavior is characterizable by tests. They do **not** generalize to: massively parallel industrial migrations (different economics — though the Google and Bloomberg precedents suggest the heuristic-capture finding may scale); to safety-critical certification regimes, where the oracle-plus-Dafny combination here is far short of what certification demands; to languages with pervasive undefined behavior or heavy hardware coupling; to the LLM landscape beyond the models of 2026–28, which may shift the BASE arm's baseline entirely; and to strong causal claims about *other researchers*, since skill, motivation, and verification aptitude are folded irrecoverably into every number. The honest summary the design itself endorses: this study's results are attributable statements about one well-instrumented case, offered as evidence and template — not as population estimates.

## Where the study stands
